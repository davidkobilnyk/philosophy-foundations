# Ten Real-Life Decisions Within Families

Decisions among people you did not choose and cannot resign from, where the
same history is remembered differently by everyone at the table. Same format as
the companion files: the situation, the live options, and the tension that keeps
the choice from resolving.

---

## 1. Who does the caring, and who pays for it

Your father needs daily help. You live twenty minutes away; your brother lives
abroad and earns three times what you do; your sister has small children.

- **Options:** split the work equally; whoever is nearest does it; those who
  cannot be present pay for professional care; hire help from his savings.
- **What makes it hard:** proximity is not a choice about willingness, but it
  becomes one in practice — and money is much easier to divide evenly than
  Tuesday afternoons.

## 2. Dividing an estate unequally

One child stayed and did the caring. One has far less money. One was given a
deposit for a house fifteen years ago and remembers it differently than you do.

- **Options:** split equally; adjust for care given; adjust for need; split
  equally and settle the imbalance in private.
- **What makes it hard:** an equal split is the only division that requires no
  justification, which is exactly why it can feel like the least fair one.

## 3. Money to an adult child or sibling, again

It is the fourth time. The need is genuine. You can afford it and you are
beginning to think the money is what keeps the situation stable.

- **Options:** give it; give it and say it is the last time; lend it formally;
  refuse and offer help of another kind.
- **What makes it hard:** you cannot tell from inside whether you are the safety
  net that lets them recover or the reason they never have to.

## 4. A family secret you did not create

You learn that a parent had another child, or that a cousin's father is not who
they think, or that a relative is seriously ill and has told only you.

- **Options:** tell the person affected; tell the family; keep the confidence;
  press the person who told you to disclose it themselves.
- **What makes it hard:** the secret is someone else's to tell, and the person
  most entitled to know it is usually the only one not being consulted.

## 5. Whose family, and for how long

Two sets of parents, one set of holidays, a partner whose family expects the
full week, and a limited number of days.

- **Options:** alternate years; split each holiday; host everyone; stop going
  and start your own tradition.
- **What makes it hard:** every arrangement reads as a ranking to somebody, and
  the family with the loudest expectations usually ends up setting the schedule
  for everyone else.

## 6. How your sibling raises their child

You think the shouting, or the screen time, or the rigidity is doing real
damage. It is not abuse. It is not your child.

- **Options:** say nothing; raise it once, privately; keep raising it; support
  the child directly without going through the parent.
- **What makes it hard:** parents are owed deference about their own children,
  and that deference is precisely what makes a child's situation invisible until
  it is severe.

## 7. A relative's addiction

They have relapsed again. Helping has not worked, and withdrawing entirely
means they lose the last person still answering the phone.

- **Options:** fund treatment; set conditions for contact; keep contact and
  refuse money; step back completely.
- **What makes it hard:** support and enabling are described the same way from
  the outside, and the difference only becomes clear long after you have had to
  choose.

## 8. The estranged parent who reappears

Eleven years of nothing, and now a letter. They are older, possibly unwell, and
would like to meet your children.

- **Options:** meet alone first; reply with conditions; introduce the children;
  do not answer.
- **What makes it hard:** you can forgive on your own behalf, but introducing
  your children commits them to a relationship whose risks only you remember.

## 9. The DNA test result

A commercial ancestry test surfaces a half-sibling nobody knew about, or shows
that a parent is not a biological parent.

- **Options:** make contact; tell your parents you know; tell nobody; answer
  only if the other person reaches out first.
- **What makes it hard:** the information is equally true for several people who
  never asked for it, and you cannot un-tell any of them once you have decided
  on their behalf that they should know.

## 10. Keeping a tradition you no longer believe

Your parents want the baptism, the bar mitzvah, the church wedding. You left the
faith years ago; they are old and this matters enormously to them.

- **Options:** do it for them; do a secular version; decline warmly; take part
  without participating in the vows.
- **What makes it hard:** going along costs you a small dishonesty about
  something that is supposed to be exactly the sort of thing one does not lie
  about — and refusing spends real happiness at the end of someone's life to
  buy it.

---

## Why these ten

They separate the difficulties peculiar to families, as distinct from the
general decisions in `real-life-decisions.md`:

- **Obligations nobody agreed to** — 1, 3, and 7 all turn on duties that arrive
  by relationship rather than by consent, and that have no natural limit.
- **Fairness against history** — 2 and 5 are about dividing something finite
  among people who each hold a different account of what came before.
- **Information that belongs to several people at once** — 4 and 9 make you the
  accidental custodian of something that is not yours to control.
- **Deciding for someone who cannot yet object** — 6, 8, and 10 all involve a
  child or an elder whose interests you are asserting on their behalf.
