# Ten Real-Life Decisions About Technology and Privacy

Decisions about tools where the people most affected are usually not in the
room, and often do not know the decision was made. Same format as the companion
files: the situation, the live options, and the tension that keeps the choice
from resolving.

---

## 1. The always-on microphone in a shared room

The speaker is genuinely useful. It sits in a kitchen where your family talks,
your friends visit, and your children's friends are looked after.

- **Options:** keep it and mute it selectively; keep it and tell visitors; move
  it somewhere private; do without.
- **What makes it hard:** you can consent for yourself in your own house, and
  everyone else in the room is subject to a decision they were never offered.

## 2. Handing over your address book

The app wants your contacts. Granting it improves the service immediately and
uploads the phone numbers of two hundred people who have not been asked.

- **Options:** grant it; grant it and delete afterward; decline and use the
  service without it; do not use the service.
- **What makes it hard:** the data is only valuable because it is about other
  people, and you are the only person in a position to give it away.

## 3. The genetic test that is also your relatives' data

Your sequence is largely your siblings', your parents', and your children's. A
match can identify a cousin who never took a test.

- **Options:** test with results kept private; test and opt out of matching;
  ask your immediate family first; do not test.
- **What makes it hard:** it is unambiguously your own body and unavoidably
  their information, and there is no version of consent that covers a family.

## 4. Reading your child's messages

You can see everything on their phone. They are thirteen. There is a real risk
you would catch, and a relationship you would be spending to catch it.

- **Options:** full visibility, disclosed; spot checks, disclosed; monitor
  covertly; do not monitor and talk instead.
- **What makes it hard:** the surveillance that would find the danger is the
  same surveillance that teaches them not to tell you things — and by the age
  where it matters most, they can route around it.

## 5. Location sharing that started as convenience

You and your partner share locations. It began as logistics. Now checking is
routine, and turning it off would itself be a statement.

- **Options:** keep it; turn it off mutually and say why; keep it and stop
  looking; make it temporary and opt-in.
- **What makes it hard:** it was agreed freely and is now a thing that cannot be
  withdrawn without meaning something, which is a strange property for consent
  to have.

## 6. Passing off what the machine wrote

The tool produced the memo, the code, the application essay. It is good. Nobody
asked how it was made and the answer would change how it is received.

- **Options:** disclose; use it for drafts and rewrite; use it where the output
  is what matters and not where the process is; do not use it.
- **What makes it hard:** the question of what "your own work" means is settled
  differently by different readers, and you are the only one who knows there is
  a question.

## 7. The free service that sells you

It costs nothing, works well, and funds itself by collecting and brokering
everything you do. The paid alternative is worse and you would have to move.

- **Options:** pay for the alternative; use the free one deliberately and limit
  what you feed it; use it without limits; leave the category entirely.
- **What makes it hard:** consent given by everyone with no real alternative is
  not obviously consent — and treating it as a personal purity decision is
  exactly how a structural problem stays personal.

## 8. Building the thing that replaces your team

You have been asked to automate work currently done by eight people, some of
whom you like, all of whom will find out later.

- **Options:** build it; build it and argue for redeployment; tell your
  colleagues; decline.
- **What makes it hard:** the tool will exist whether or not you build it, and
  being the one who builds it is the only position from which the transition
  might be made survivable.

## 9. Posting the group photo

Everyone in it had a good time. Two of them are careful about being online, one
is a teacher, and nobody was asked.

- **Options:** post it; ask everyone first; post with faces obscured; send it to
  them privately instead.
- **What makes it hard:** asking permission for every photograph is socially
  impossible, and the cost of not asking falls entirely on whoever had the
  strongest reason to be asked.

## 10. Work software on your own phone

The job requires the management profile, which brings mail and calendar and
also remote wipe, location, and inventory of what else is installed.

- **Options:** install it and accept the reach; carry a separate work device;
  negotiate limits in writing; refuse and lose the flexibility.
- **What makes it hard:** you can decline in principle and not in practice, and
  the thing you are trading is not really access to a phone but the boundary
  between two parts of your life.

---

## Why these ten

They separate the difficulties that get flattened into "privacy":

- **Data about people who are not in the decision** — 1, 2, 3, and 9 all
  dispose of information belonging to someone who was never asked.
- **Consent that is not really available** — 5, 7, and 10 are agreed to freely
  in form and unrefusable in practice.
- **Authority over someone else's privacy** — 4 is the case where you have both
  the right and the power, and using them costs the thing they were for.
- **What counts as your own** — 6 and 8 ask what remains yours (your work, your
  job) once a tool can do the part that used to identify it as yours.
