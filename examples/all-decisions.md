# All Real-Life Decisions

Every decision from the fifteen files in this directory, collected in one place
and numbered continuously. Each entry names its category in the heading, so a
single decision can be quoted or extracted without losing where it came from.
The per-category files remain the source of truth; this is a compilation.

Each entry gives the situation, the live options, and **what makes it hard** —
the tension that keeps the choice from resolving into an obvious answer. The
axes column below records what each category's cases were chosen to span; the
full reasoning is in that category's own file.

## General

*Source: `real-life-decisions.md`*

### 1. General — Taking the job that pays more but means moving away

You are offered a role in another city with a meaningful raise and better
long-term prospects. Your parents are aging nearby, and your closest friendships
are where you live now.

- **Options:** accept and move; decline and stay; negotiate remote or delayed start.
- **What makes it hard:** money and career growth are measurable; proximity to
  people you love is not. The costs of leaving arrive slowly and are hard to
  reverse.

### 2. General — Whether to have children, and when

You and your partner disagree about timing, or you are unsure whether you want
children at all. Fertility, finances, and career timelines all push in different
directions.

- **Options:** try now; wait a defined number of years; decide not to; freeze
  eggs or embryos to keep the option open.
- **What makes it hard:** you are choosing on behalf of a person who does not
  exist yet, and the preferences you will have afterward are not the ones you
  have now.

### 3. General — Moving a parent into assisted living

Your mother is becoming unsafe living alone, but insists she is fine and does
not want to leave her house.

- **Options:** respect her stated wish; move her against her objection; bring in
  in-home care; move her in with you.
- **What makes it hard:** her autonomy and her safety point opposite ways, and
  you are also weighing your own capacity without wanting to admit that you are.

### 4. General — Reporting a colleague's misconduct

You witness a coworker falsifying results, expensing personal costs, or
mistreating a junior employee. Reporting it will likely be traced back to you.

- **Options:** report through official channels; raise it with the person
  directly; tell a trusted manager informally; say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** the harm is real but diffuse, and the cost to you is
  concrete and personal. Loyalty to a person conflicts with loyalty to everyone
  the misconduct touches.

### 5. General — How aggressively to treat a serious illness

You or someone you are responsible for is diagnosed with a condition where
treatment offers a modest chance of more time at a significant cost in
suffering.

- **Options:** pursue aggressive treatment; choose palliative care; try one
  round and reassess.
- **What makes it hard:** more time and better time are different goods, and
  the person best placed to weigh them may be too sick or too frightened to
  decide.

### 6. General — Cutting off a family member

A sibling or parent has repeatedly caused you harm and shows no sign of
changing. Contact costs you something real each time.

- **Options:** cut contact entirely; set strict limited contact; keep trying;
  attempt a mediated conversation.
- **What makes it hard:** obligations to family are not chosen, and the door you
  close may not reopen. You are also deciding for others in the family who will
  have to take sides.

### 7. General — Leaving a stable career to do the thing you actually want

You have savings for two years and a skill you have never tested commercially.
Staying is safe and quietly deadening.

- **Options:** quit and commit; go part-time and build on the side; set a
  savings threshold and a deadline; stay.
- **What makes it hard:** you cannot learn whether it would work without giving
  up the thing that makes failure survivable.

## Vegan

*Source: `vegan-decisions.md`*

### 8. Vegan — The holiday meal a relative cooked for you

Your aunt spent the afternoon making a dish she believes is vegan. Partway
through you realize it contains butter, or chicken stock, or honey.

- **Options:** eat it and say nothing; eat it and mention it afterward; decline
  quietly; decline and explain in the moment.
- **What makes it hard:** the animal was harmed whether or not you eat this
  plate. What is left to decide is what your refusal signals — a standard held,
  or an insult to someone who tried.

### 9. Vegan — Medication that is animal-derived or animal-tested

The drug you need contains gelatin, or lactose, or was necessarily tested on
animals. In some cases no vegan-formulated equivalent exists.

- **Options:** take it; find a compounding pharmacy or alternative formulation;
  refuse and accept the health cost; take it and offset by donating.
- **What makes it hard:** refusing does not undo the testing already done, and
  your own health is itself something you have obligations about — to
  dependents, and arguably to the cause you can only serve while well.

### 10. Vegan — Feeding a cat

Cats are obligate carnivores. Keeping one healthy means buying the bodies of
other animals, often many more of them, by weight, than you would have eaten.

- **Options:** feed conventional meat-based food; try a supplemented
  plant-based formulation under veterinary supervision; rehome the cat; adopt
  only herbivorous animals in future.
- **What makes it hard:** you took on a duty of care to this specific animal,
  and discharging it requires funding exactly the industry you object to.

### 11. Vegan — The leather boots you already own

You went vegan three years ago. The boots predate it, they still fit, and
replacing them means buying new synthetic ones.

- **Options:** wear them out; stop wearing them and donate them; stop wearing
  them and discard them; wear them but never buy leather again.
- **What makes it hard:** if the objection is to funding harm, wearing them
  funds nothing and discarding them wastes an animal's life twice. If the
  objection is to treating an animal as material, wearing them is the problem
  regardless of when you bought them.

### 12. Vegan — A partner who is not vegan, and one kitchen

You share a home, a fridge, a grocery budget, and sometimes a pan. They are
sympathetic but not persuaded.

- **Options:** a fully vegan household; separate shelves and cookware; cook
  animal products for them; eat separately.
- **What makes it hard:** in your own home, tolerating it looks like complicity;
  banning it looks like coercing an adult who has considered your reasons and
  disagreed.

### 13. Vegan — Eggs from rescued backyard hens

A friend keeps ex-battery hens who would otherwise have been killed. They are
well cared for, they lay regardless, and the eggs are otherwise thrown out.

- **Options:** eat them; refuse on principle; refuse because of what normalizing
  egg-eating does; feed the eggs back to the hens.
- **What makes it hard:** the standard reasons — killing, confinement, purchase
  — are all absent here. What remains is either a rule you keep because rules
  that bend stop working, or nothing at all.

### 14. Vegan — Taking the job at a company with an animal-products line

The role is good, the pay is good, and 8% of the company's revenue comes from
a division you object to. Or the client list includes a dairy conglomerate.

- **Options:** take it; take it and refuse to staff that account; negotiate an
  exemption in writing; decline and say why.
- **What makes it hard:** almost every large employer touches this somewhere, so
  a consistent rule may leave you unemployable — and your leverage to change
  anything is greater inside than outside.

### 15. Vegan — Welfare reform or abolition, when you can only fund one

You have money to give. One organization campaigns for larger cages and
stunning requirements; another refuses anything short of ending use entirely.

- **Options:** fund welfare reform; fund abolition; split; fund alternative
  protein research instead.
- **What makes it hard:** the reform demonstrably reduces suffering now and may
  entrench the institution by making it look acceptable. You are betting on a
  causal story about the next fifty years that nobody can check.

### 16. Vegan — How hard to push at a work dinner

A colleague asks, in front of eight people, why you do not eat meat. There is a
real answer and it is not a comfortable one.

- **Options:** deflect with a health reason; give the honest reason briefly;
  make the full argument; say nothing and change the subject.
- **What makes it hard:** the case is strongest when it is stated plainly, and
  stating it plainly implies a judgment about everyone at the table — which is
  also the most reliable way to make them stop listening.

## Boycott

*Source: `boycott-decisions.md`*

### 17. Boycott — Boycotting a company you cannot actually avoid

The target is a near-monopoly: the online retailer that delivers where you
live, the search engine and phone platform, the single grocery chain in town.
Doing without it costs you hours, money, or access you cannot spare.

- **Options:** boycott fully and absorb the cost; use it only where no
  substitute exists; keep using it and act through other channels; do nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** a boycott only means something if it is a real
  withdrawal, and the companies most worth boycotting are exactly the ones
  structured so that withdrawal is hardest.

### 18. Boycott — When the boycott hurts the workers it is meant to help

You stop buying from a garment brand over its factory conditions. If enough
people do, the plant closes and the workers lose the wage they were fighting to
improve, with nothing better waiting.

- **Options:** boycott anyway; boycott only if the workers themselves have
  called for it; campaign for reform without withholding purchases; fund the
  union directly.
- **What makes it hard:** the people you are acting for are the people who pay
  first, and they may not agree that the trade is worth it.

### 19. Boycott — Divesting money you do not directly control

Your retirement savings sit in an index fund that holds the company. Screening
it out means a worse-diversified portfolio, higher fees, or leaving an employer
match on the table.

- **Options:** switch to a screened fund; accept the fee and tracking cost;
  hold and offset with donations; leave it and focus elsewhere.
- **What makes it hard:** selling a share on the secondary market takes no money
  from the company — someone else simply buys it. The effect, if any, is on
  price signals and legitimacy, not on the firm's bank balance.

### 20. Boycott — Selling the stock or keeping it to vote

You could divest, or you could hold the shares precisely so you can file
resolutions, vote against the board, and force disclosure.

- **Options:** divest and say so publicly; hold and vote; hold a token stake for
  standing and divest the rest; join a shareholder coalition.
- **What makes it hard:** divesting keeps your hands clean and hands the vote to
  someone who does not care. Staying keeps the leverage and makes you, on paper,
  an owner of the thing you object to.

### 21. Boycott — A boycott you think is aimed at the wrong target

People you respect and generally stand with have called for one. You think the
factual case against this particular company is weak, or that it has been
singled out arbitrarily.

- **Options:** join in solidarity; decline and say why; decline quietly; join
  while arguing internally to redirect it.
- **What makes it hard:** movements need people who show up without relitigating
  every call, and they also need someone willing to say the evidence is thin
  before a company is damaged over it.

### 22. Boycott — The shop that stocks the brand

Your target is a manufacturer. The only place selling it locally is an
independent store whose owner has no say over the supply chain and would be
badly hurt by losing your business.

- **Options:** boycott the store too; buy everything there except that brand;
  tell the owner why; leave the store alone and target the manufacturer.
- **What makes it hard:** pressure travels up the chain only if intermediaries
  feel it, and the intermediaries who feel it most are the ones least able to
  do anything.

## Activism

*Source: `political-activism-decisions.md`*

### 23. Activism — Breaking a law you believe is unjust

The blockade, the occupation, the trespass. It would be effective, it is
illegal, and you have a job, a visa, or children who depend on you.

- **Options:** take the arrest openly; participate in ways that stay legal;
  support those who do it without doing it yourself; stay out.
- **What makes it hard:** civil disobedience draws its moral force from
  accepting the penalty publicly — which means the people best placed to be
  arrested are those with the least to lose, and their arrest carries the
  least weight.

### 24. Activism — Tactics that work by making people angry

Blocking a motorway, disrupting a performance, defacing a monument. Coverage is
enormous. Polling on your cause gets worse.

- **Options:** use disruptive tactics; use them only against the responsible
  institution; stick to persuasion and lobbying; do both through separate
  groups.
- **What makes it hard:** attention and sympathy are different currencies, and
  nobody can reliably tell you the exchange rate in advance — the historical
  cases are read both ways by serious people.

### 25. Activism — Voting for the candidate you can live with

Your preferred candidate cannot win. The one who can is materially better than
the alternative and has a record you find genuinely objectionable.

- **Options:** vote for the viable candidate; vote third party; abstain; vote
  viable but organize publicly against their record.
- **What makes it hard:** treating your vote as an instrument makes it almost
  worthless individually; treating it as a statement makes it worthless
  instrumentally. Both descriptions are true at once.

### 26. Activism — Money from a donor you would not defend

A large gift would fund three organizers for two years. The donor made the
money in a way your movement exists to oppose, or wants a say in strategy.

- **Options:** take it with conditions in writing; take it and disclose the
  source; refuse; take it and route it through an independent fund.
- **What makes it hard:** refusing keeps you clean and small. Accepting funds
  the work and gives your opponents an accurate story about who pays for you.

### 27. Activism — A coalition with people whose other positions you reject

To win this vote you need a bloc that agrees with you here and holds views you
find repugnant elsewhere. Without them the measure fails.

- **Options:** form the coalition openly; cooperate tacitly without joint
  appearances; refuse; work with them on this and campaign against them
  elsewhere.
- **What makes it hard:** coalitions launder reputations in both directions,
  and the people your alliance costs most are usually not the ones deciding
  whether to make it.

### 28. Activism — The compromise bill

The legislation delivers 40% of what you demanded, and its passage will drain
the urgency that made the other 60% possible. Opposing it means people go
without the 40% now.

- **Options:** back it and keep organizing; back it and say publicly it is
  inadequate; oppose it; stay neutral and let others decide.
- **What makes it hard:** you are weighing a certain, immediate, partial gain
  against a speculative larger one, and the people who need it now are not the
  same people who would benefit later.

### 29. Activism — Staying in an organization that has gone wrong

The leadership has become unaccountable, or has taken a position you think
betrays the point. You have ten years of relationships inside it.

- **Options:** organize internally for change; resign quietly; resign publicly
  with a statement; stay and dissent openly.
- **What makes it hard:** leaving publicly hands ammunition to opponents who
  want the whole thing discredited; staying quietly makes you a name on the
  masthead that lends it credibility.

### 30. Activism — Leaking what you agreed not to disclose

You have documents that would prove the thing your campaign has alleged for
years. You signed a confidentiality agreement, and the leak would be traced to
a small number of people.

- **Options:** leak anonymously; leak on the record; give it to a regulator;
  hold it and pursue lawful routes.
- **What makes it hard:** the promise was real and freely given, and keeping it
  protects a wrong that the promise was arguably designed to protect.

### 31. Activism — Organizing full time or earning and giving

You could organize for a modest salary, or take the high-paying job and fund
three organizers a year with the difference.

- **Options:** organize; earn and give; earn and give while volunteering;
  organize now and switch later.
- **What makes it hard:** money is fungible and your presence is not — but
  movements are made of people who stayed, and it is very easy to be persuaded
  that the comfortable option is the effective one.

## Daily life

*Source: `daily-lifestyle-decisions.md`*

### 32. Daily life — The phone in the bedroom

It is the alarm, the reading light, the thing you check at 6:40am before you
are properly awake, and the last hour of most evenings.

- **Options:** charge it in another room; keep it and use limits or greyscale;
  keep it and change nothing; a separate alarm clock and no phone after 10pm.
- **What makes it hard:** the cost is invisible per instance and only shows up
  in aggregate — and the device that would show you the aggregate is the one
  under examination.

### 33. Daily life — Keeping a car

It costs a few hundred a month standing still. Transit would add forty minutes
a day but reclaim the parking, the insurance, and the maintenance.

- **Options:** keep it; sell it and use transit plus occasional rental; keep it
  and use it less; downsize to something cheaper.
- **What makes it hard:** the car buys time and buys freedom on the handful of
  days you really need it, and you cannot know in advance which days those are.

### 34. Daily life — How much news to take in

Staying informed is a civic obligation of some kind. Six hours a week of it
leaves you anxious and no better able to act on any of it.

- **Options:** a daily briefing and nothing else; a weekly long-form roundup;
  follow only what you can act on; stay fully current.
- **What makes it hard:** the fraction of the news you can do anything about is
  tiny, but you cannot identify which fraction that is without reading it, and
  disengagement is exactly what makes people easy to govern badly.

### 35. Daily life — Answering work messages after hours

Nobody has told you to. Replying at 9pm is noticed and rewarded. Not replying
is also noticed.

- **Options:** hard cutoff and say so; reply only to genuine emergencies; stay
  responsive; batch replies to one evening check.
- **What makes it hard:** a boundary only holds if you pay the cost of holding
  it, and the cost lands on you while the benefit of your availability is
  spread across people who will not remember it at review time.

### 36. Daily life — Declining the invitation

You are tired. It is the third thing this week. You would enjoy it once you
were there, and you would also enjoy the evening at home.

- **Options:** go; decline honestly; decline with an excuse; go and leave early.
- **What makes it hard:** friendships are maintained by showing up more or less
  reliably, and the specific evening you skip is never the one that matters —
  until enough of them accumulate.

### 37. Daily life — Whether to drink at all

Two or three drinks a week is socially frictionless and probably not costing
you much. Zero is cleaner, and makes every dinner a small conversation.

- **Options:** stop entirely; keep it to social occasions; set a weekly cap;
  change nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** the marginal harm at low volumes is genuinely
  uncertain, and abstaining has a social cost that falls entirely on you while
  the benefit is diffuse and statistical.

### 38. Daily life — What to post about your family

The photos are good, the people who love your kids are far away, and your
children cannot consent to a permanent public record of their childhood.

- **Options:** post freely; a private list only; no faces or names; nothing at
  all, and send photos directly.
- **What makes it hard:** you are making a durable decision on behalf of someone
  who will have their own view about it in fifteen years, in exchange for
  something you enjoy now.

### 39. Daily life — Cheap now or durable later

The £40 version will last two years. The £180 version will last fifteen, if you
still want it in fifteen years and if the claim is even true.

- **Options:** buy cheap and replace; buy the expensive one; buy secondhand;
  buy nothing and borrow.
- **What makes it hard:** buying well is cheaper over time only if you can
  afford the outlay and your tastes hold — and the pitch for durability is made
  by people selling durability.

### 40. Daily life — Living alone or with other people

Alone is quiet, and yours, and expensive. Sharing halves the rent and fills the
kitchen with someone else's schedule.

- **Options:** live alone; share with friends; share with strangers; live with
  family.
- **What makes it hard:** solitude is restorative right up to the point where it
  becomes isolation, and the transition does not announce itself.

### 41. Daily life — Where the exercise hour comes from

An hour of training has to displace an hour of something: sleep, work, the
family, the evening.

- **Options:** early morning at the cost of sleep; lunchtime at the cost of
  work; evening at the cost of family time; shorter sessions more often.
- **What makes it hard:** the return is real but slow and statistical, while
  everything it displaces makes its claim on you today, specifically, by name.

## Dating

*Source: `dating-decisions.md`*

### 42. Dating — When to disclose the significant thing

The bipolar diagnosis, the herpes, the debt, the criminal record, the fact that
you do not want children. Early is fair and invites a snap judgment; late feels
like something withheld.

- **Options:** put it in the profile; say it on the first date; wait until it is
  serious; wait until they ask.
- **What makes it hard:** disclosure exists to let someone consent to what they
  are getting into, and telling them before they know you at all guarantees the
  decision is made on the label rather than on you.

### 43. Dating — How much to look them up first

Ten minutes online would tell you their employer, their politics, their ex, and
possibly a court record. It is also a one-sided briefing they did not agree to.

- **Options:** a basic safety check only; a full search; nothing beyond the
  profile; search and say that you did.
- **What makes it hard:** meeting a stranger is a real risk and checking is
  prudent — and knowing things you have no ordinary way of knowing makes the
  first hour a performance of discovering them.

### 44. Dating — Seeing several people at once, and when to say so

Nobody has raised exclusivity. You are on second dates with two people and one
of them is clearly assuming otherwise.

- **Options:** raise it now; wait until someone asks; wait until it is serious
  with one; keep it to yourself as nobody's business yet.
- **What makes it hard:** the norm is genuinely unsettled, so acting on your own
  reading is fine right up until you discover theirs was different — and by then
  someone has already been hurt.

### 45. Dating — No spark yet, three dates in

They are kind, interesting, and available. You feel nothing much. People often
say attraction grew for them over months.

- **Options:** end it now; give it three more; end it and say why honestly;
  keep seeing them while looking around.
- **What makes it hard:** chemistry sometimes builds and sometimes never does,
  and staying to find out spends their time on a maybe you would not accept
  from someone else.

### 46. Dating — They do not want what you want

Six months in and going well. They are clear they do not want marriage, or
children, or to leave this city — and you do.

- **Options:** end it now; stay and hope it changes; stay knowing it will not;
  set a deadline and revisit.
- **What makes it hard:** people do change their minds, so hoping is not
  irrational — but organizing your life around someone changing is a way of not
  believing what they told you plainly.

### 47. Dating — Dating across a real values divide

They are good to you and they hold a political or religious position you find
seriously wrong, on something that is not abstract for you.

- **Options:** end it; stay and keep it out of bounds; stay and argue it out;
  stay and see whether either of you moves.
- **What makes it hard:** treating a conviction as a dealbreaker means treating
  people as their positions, and treating it as private means living with
  someone who would vote against something you need.

### 48. Dating — Your friend's ex, or your colleague

The connection is real. She dated your close friend for two years, or he sits
four desks away and you would both still be there afterward.

- **Options:** don't; ask your friend first; go ahead and tell them after; go
  ahead and say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** asking permission gives someone a veto over your life
  they should probably not have, and not asking treats their feelings as
  something to be managed after the fact.

### 49. Dating — Moving cities for someone

Eight months in. Their job cannot move and yours can. You would arrive with no
friends, no network, and a relationship that has never been tested by anything.

- **Options:** move; ask them to move; long distance with an end date; end it.
- **What makes it hard:** whoever moves carries the dependence, and the very
  thing that would tell you whether the relationship can bear that — time — is
  what the move is meant to shortcut.

### 50. Dating — An asymmetry you did not create

You earn four times what they do, or you are their manager's peer, or their
visa depends on staying in the country and you know it.

- **Options:** proceed as equals and ignore it; name it explicitly; change what
  you can (roles, money, housing); decline on principle.
- **What makes it hard:** consent given under dependence is still consent, and
  it is also the exact circumstance in which someone finds it hardest to say no
  — including to themselves.

### 51. Dating — Saying the true unkind thing, or fading out

You are not interested. The honest reason is something about them they cannot
change and would not enjoy hearing.

- **Options:** a brief honest message; a vague but clear ending; the full
  reason if asked; stop replying.
- **What makes it hard:** fading out spares them a bad five minutes and gives
  them nothing to close on, while the truth is useful only if they wanted it,
  and you cannot ask without delivering it.

## Family

*Source: `family-decisions.md`*

### 52. Family — Dividing an estate unequally

One child stayed and did the caring. One has far less money. One was given a
deposit for a house fifteen years ago and remembers it differently than you do.

- **Options:** split equally; adjust for care given; adjust for need; split
  equally and settle the imbalance in private.
- **What makes it hard:** an equal split is the only division that requires no
  justification, which is exactly why it can feel like the least fair one.

### 53. Family — Money to an adult child or sibling, again

It is the fourth time. The need is genuine. You can afford it and you are
beginning to think the money is what keeps the situation stable.

- **Options:** give it; give it and say it is the last time; lend it formally;
  refuse and offer help of another kind.
- **What makes it hard:** you cannot tell from inside whether you are the safety
  net that lets them recover or the reason they never have to.

### 54. Family — A family secret you did not create

You learn that a parent had another child, or that a cousin's father is not who
they think, or that a relative is seriously ill and has told only you.

- **Options:** tell the person affected; tell the family; keep the confidence;
  press the person who told you to disclose it themselves.
- **What makes it hard:** the secret is someone else's to tell, and the person
  most entitled to know it is usually the only one not being consulted.

### 55. Family — Whose family, and for how long

Two sets of parents, one set of holidays, a partner whose family expects the
full week, and a limited number of days.

- **Options:** alternate years; split each holiday; host everyone; stop going
  and start your own tradition.
- **What makes it hard:** every arrangement reads as a ranking to somebody, and
  the family with the loudest expectations usually ends up setting the schedule
  for everyone else.

### 56. Family — How your sibling raises their child

You think the shouting, or the screen time, or the rigidity is doing real
damage. It is not abuse. It is not your child.

- **Options:** say nothing; raise it once, privately; keep raising it; support
  the child directly without going through the parent.
- **What makes it hard:** parents are owed deference about their own children,
  and that deference is precisely what makes a child's situation invisible until
  it is severe.

### 57. Family — A relative's addiction

They have relapsed again. Helping has not worked, and withdrawing entirely
means they lose the last person still answering the phone.

- **Options:** fund treatment; set conditions for contact; keep contact and
  refuse money; step back completely.
- **What makes it hard:** support and enabling are described the same way from
  the outside, and the difference only becomes clear long after you have had to
  choose.

### 58. Family — Keeping a tradition you no longer believe

Your parents want the baptism, the bar mitzvah, the church wedding. You left the
faith years ago; they are old and this matters enormously to them.

- **Options:** do it for them; do a secular version; decline warmly; take part
  without participating in the vows.
- **What makes it hard:** going along costs you a small dishonesty about
  something that is supposed to be exactly the sort of thing one does not lie
  about — and refusing spends real happiness at the end of someone's life to
  buy it.

## Neighbors

*Source: `neighbor-decisions.md`*

### 59. Neighbors — Noise, and whether to escalate

Music until 1am on weeknights. You have mentioned it twice, politely, and it
improves for a fortnight each time.

- **Options:** speak to them again; write to them; complain to the council or
  landlord; soundproof at your own expense.
- **What makes it hard:** the informal route preserves the relationship and has
  visibly stopped working, and the formal route solves the noise by ending any
  chance of an informal route ever again.

### 60. Neighbors — The dog

It barks all day when they are out, or it is loose and has run at your child.
The owners are attached to it and defensive.

- **Options:** raise it with them; report it; document incidents in case it
  escalates; adjust your own routine around it.
- **What makes it hard:** reporting an animal can end with it destroyed, and not
  reporting one means the record is empty on the day it bites someone.

### 61. Neighbors — Reporting something official

The loft conversion has no permit, or the number of people living in the flat
breaches the lease, or you suspect a child or an elderly person there is not
being cared for.

- **Options:** report it; ask them about it first; report only the welfare
  concern; do nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** the rules exist because self-interested neighbors are
  unreliable judges, and you are the only person positioned to see this — which
  makes you both the right witness and an interested party.

### 62. Neighbors — The neighbor who may not be coping

The bins have not moved in three weeks. The curtains stay shut. She is
seventy-eight, proud, and has told you before that she manages fine.

- **Options:** knock and ask directly; contact her family; call social services
  or a welfare line; keep an eye and do nothing yet.
- **What makes it hard:** intruding on a competent adult's privacy is a real
  wrong, and waiting for certainty means the intervention arrives after the
  fall rather than before it.

### 63. Neighbors — The short-term let next door

The flat has become a rotating stream of strangers with suitcases at midnight,
or a home business with vans blocking the street. It is their property and
their income.

- **Options:** talk to the owner; report it to the platform or council; organize
  neighbors collectively; accept it.
- **What makes it hard:** they are making legitimate use of what they own, and
  what you are protecting — a street where you know who lives there — is real
  but has no standing anyone has to recognize.

### 64. Neighbors — The shared repair they will not pay for

The party wall, the fence, the shared drain. Your half of the cost is
manageable; they say they cannot afford it, or simply will not engage.

- **Options:** pay the whole thing yourself; pursue them formally; do the
  minimum patch; leave it and let it deteriorate.
- **What makes it hard:** paying for both preserves the peace and establishes,
  for every future repair, that you are the one who pays.

### 65. Neighbors — The neighborhood group chat

What started as parcel deliveries is now photographs of strangers walking past,
speculation about who does not belong, and talk of calling the police.

- **Options:** push back in the chat; leave quietly; stay and moderate what you
  can; start something separate.
- **What makes it hard:** the group genuinely does help people, and objecting
  makes you the neighbor who is difficult about safety — which is why the drift
  usually goes unchallenged.

### 66. Neighbors — Opposing the development

Forty new flats behind your garden. It would take your light and your view; it
would also house forty households in a place with nowhere to live.

- **Options:** object formally; object only to the height and massing; support
  it; stay out of it.
- **What makes it hard:** your objection would be sincere and your interest is
  obvious, and the mechanism that lets you object is the same one that has made
  housing scarce for the people who would live there.

### 67. Neighbors — Where your camera points

The doorbell camera covers your porch, most of the pavement, and their front
door. It has already been useful once.

- **Options:** keep it as is; angle it to your property only; tell them it is
  there; take it down.
- **What makes it hard:** security only works by recording the approach, and the
  approach is where other people's ordinary comings and goings happen.

## Workplace

*Source: `workplace-decisions.md`*

### 68. Workplace — Telling colleagues what you earn

You have discovered you are paid considerably more than someone doing the same
work, or considerably less. Saying so would change what everyone can negotiate.

- **Options:** tell the person affected; tell the team; raise it with your
  manager instead; say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** secrecy about pay serves the employer almost entirely,
  and breaking it hands someone a grievance they cannot unlearn and may not be
  able to act on.

### 69. Workplace — Negotiating hard from a fixed pool

You have an outside offer and real leverage. The raise budget for your team is
capped, and what you win comes out of what is left for four other people.

- **Options:** negotiate to the maximum; take a fair number and leave room;
  negotiate hard and push for the pool to be raised; accept what is offered.
- **What makes it hard:** you are the only person in the room with an interest
  in your salary, and the zero-sum framing is one your employer chose and
  benefits from.

### 70. Workplace — The honest reference

A former report you had to manage out asks you for a reference. They tried
hard, they were not good at the job, and they need work.

- **Options:** decline to give one; give a narrow factual confirmation; give a
  warm reference; be candid about the fit.
- **What makes it hard:** a reference is only worth anything if it is honest,
  and the cost of your honesty falls on one identifiable person while the
  benefit is spread across a hiring process that will never know it.

### 71. Workplace — Firing someone who is trying

Six months of support, a clear plan, visible effort, and the work is still not
good enough. They have a mortgage and they know it is going badly.

- **Options:** terminate now; extend the plan; move them to a role that fits;
  keep them and absorb the gap.
- **What makes it hard:** effort is the thing you spend a career telling people
  to give, and it is not what the job is actually paid for — and every month you
  delay is a month they are not looking elsewhere.

### 72. Workplace — Telling your manager you are interviewing

You are two rounds into a process elsewhere. Your manager is about to staff you
onto a year-long project, and has been decent to you.

- **Options:** tell them now; tell them when you have an offer; say nothing and
  decline the project vaguely; take the project and leave if it comes through.
- **What makes it hard:** honesty here is unilateral — they would not tell you
  in advance that your role was being cut, and disclosing early can make the
  decision for you.

### 73. Workplace — Covering for a teammate in crisis

Their work has slipped badly for two months. There is a divorce, or an illness.
You have been quietly redoing parts of it.

- **Options:** keep covering; tell them you cannot continue; tell your manager;
  ask them what they want you to do.
- **What makes it hard:** covering protects them now and delays the help that
  actually fits — and the person paying for it, invisibly, is you.

### 74. Workplace — Building the thing that is legal and bad

The cancellation flow with the buried button, the metric everyone knows
overstates the result, the fee disclosed on page eleven. Nothing here is
unlawful.

- **Options:** build it; build it and record your objection; propose a version
  you can defend; refuse.
- **What makes it hard:** refusing on your own achieves nothing but replacement
  by someone quieter, and "it is legal" is doing an enormous amount of work in
  a sentence nobody wants to finish.

### 75. Workplace — The promotion into management

More money, more security, and an end to the work you are actually good at and
enjoy. Declining it marks you as someone with no ambition.

- **Options:** take it; decline and stay technical; take it for a fixed trial;
  ask for a senior individual track instead.
- **What makes it hard:** organizations mostly offer one ladder, so refusing the
  role often means refusing the pay — and you cannot find out whether you would
  be good at it without giving up the thing you would return to.

### 76. Workplace — Owning the mistake nobody noticed

Your error cost real money, or shipped a wrong number to a client. It has not
been traced to you and probably never will be.

- **Options:** report it immediately; fix it quietly and say nothing; tell your
  manager privately; disclose it to whoever was affected.
- **What makes it hard:** disclosure is straightforwardly right and lands
  entirely on you, and the strongest argument for silence — that the harm is
  already done and confessing helps nobody — is the same argument available to
  everyone who has ever concealed anything.

## Hobbies

*Source: `hobby-decisions.md`*

### 77. Hobbies — Turning it into work

People are offering to pay. You could take commissions, sell the surplus, go
part-time at the job and make up the difference.

- **Options:** keep it unpaid; take occasional paid work; go semi-professional;
  make it the career.
- **What makes it hard:** the thing you love about it is that nobody is owed
  anything, and payment brings deadlines, customers, and the obligation to make
  the version that sells rather than the version you wanted.

### 78. Hobbies — The gear you might not use

The better instrument, the carbon frame, the proper kiln. It would remove a real
limitation, cost a month's savings, and might sit unused by March.

- **Options:** buy it now; buy secondhand; rent or borrow first; earn it by
  hitting a defined milestone.
- **What makes it hard:** equipment is the most reliable way to feel like you
  are progressing without progressing, and it is also sometimes exactly what is
  holding you back.

### 79. Hobbies — Competing or not

There are gradings, leagues, open calls, races. Entering would sharpen you fast
and turn the thing into a scoreboard.

- **Options:** compete seriously; enter occasionally for the deadline; set
  private goals instead; refuse the whole frame.
- **What makes it hard:** measurement is the fastest route to getting better and
  the most reliable route to only valuing what can be measured.

### 80. Hobbies — Quitting after twelve years

You no longer look forward to it. You are also good at it, known for it, and it
is part of how you and everyone else describe who you are.

- **Options:** stop; take an open-ended break; reduce it to something small;
  keep going and wait for it to come back.
- **What makes it hard:** identity outlives enthusiasm, so quitting costs
  something real that has nothing to do with whether you still enjoy it — and
  fallow periods are ordinary and do end.

### 81. Hobbies — The hours it takes from the people you live with

Six hours a week of training, or rehearsal, or the workshop. It is the thing
that makes you tolerable, and it is six hours nobody else in the house gets.

- **Options:** keep the time and defend it; halve it; move it to hours nobody
  wants anyway; involve them in it.
- **What makes it hard:** a person with nothing of their own is worse to live
  with, and that argument is also the most convenient thing you could possibly
  believe.

### 82. Hobbies — Shortcuts, and what counts as yours

The generated draft, the kit, the tracing, the pre-cut joinery, the assisted
edit. It gets you a better result faster and you did not do all of it.

- **Options:** avoid them entirely; use them and disclose; use them for the
  parts you do not care about; use whatever works and judge only the result.
- **What makes it hard:** if the point is the object, the shortcut is obviously
  fine; if the point is the doing, it is obviously not — and most people want
  both without having decided which is primary.

### 83. Hobbies — Posting it

You could put the work online. Feedback would improve it and an audience would
keep you going. It would also mean making things that do well online.

- **Options:** post everything; post selectively; share only with a small group;
  keep it private.
- **What makes it hard:** an audience is a genuine spur and a slow editor of
  what you attempt, and you will not notice it editing you.

### 84. Hobbies — The only club within thirty miles

It is the one place to do this locally. The culture is cliquey, or casually
unpleasant to newcomers, or run by someone nobody will confront.

- **Options:** join and keep your head down; join and try to change it; travel
  further; do it alone.
- **What makes it hard:** most hobbies need other people to be any good at all,
  and leaving costs you the activity while costing the culture nothing.

### 85. Hobbies — The risky one, now that people depend on you

Climbing, motorcycles, freediving, solo expeditions. The risk was always there
and now there is a child, or a partner who cannot sleep when you are out.

- **Options:** stop; reduce the risk with rules and equipment; keep going and
  insure heavily; keep going unchanged.
- **What makes it hard:** you are entitled to accept risk to your own life, and
  the consequences of the bad outcome fall almost entirely on people who did not
  choose it.

## Money

*Source: `money-decisions.md`*

### 86. Money — How much insurance to carry

Every policy is, on average, a losing bet: the premium exceeds the expected
payout, or the insurer would not sell it. You are choosing how much to lose on
purpose.

- **Options:** insure heavily; insure only against ruin; take the high
  deductible and self-insure the rest; skip the optional cover entirely.
- **What makes it hard:** the correct amount depends on which losses would end
  you rather than merely hurt, and people are systematically bad at telling
  those apart until they are in one.

### 87. Money — Paying down the debt or investing the difference

The loan is at 4%. The market has historically returned more. Clearing it is
guaranteed; the spread is not.

- **Options:** overpay the debt; invest and keep the debt; split; clear the
  high-interest debt only and invest the rest.
- **What makes it hard:** the arithmetic favors investing and the arithmetic
  assumes you will hold your nerve through the years where it does not work —
  which is a claim about you, not about markets.

### 88. Money — How much risk, in a life you only run once

The aggressive allocation has the better expected outcome across many
lifetimes. You get one, and it has to cover a specific set of years.

- **Options:** maximize expected return; hold a large safe allocation; glide
  from one to the other with age; decide by what you could stand to lose.
- **What makes it hard:** expected value is the right guide for a bet you can
  repeat, and nothing about a retirement is repeatable.

### 89. Money — The emergency fund that is quietly shrinking

Six months of expenses in cash, losing to inflation every year, doing nothing —
except the one thing it is for.

- **Options:** hold six months; hold three and a credit line; invest it and
  accept the sequence risk; size it to your actual job security.
- **What makes it hard:** the cost is continuous and visible while the benefit
  is a single event that may never come, which is exactly the shape of decision
  people abandon just before they need it.

### 90. Money — Cash or equity

The startup offers a lower salary and a slice that is probably worth nothing
and might be worth a house.

- **Options:** take the cash; take the equity; take a mix; take the cash and
  invest the difference yourself.
- **What makes it hard:** the equity's expected value can be genuinely high
  while its median outcome is zero, and you cannot diversify a bet that is also
  your employer, your salary, and your next reference.

### 91. Money — Walking away from a debt you legally owe

Bankruptcy, or a strategic default, or simply stopping. It is lawful, it is
what the system provides for, and someone will not be paid.

- **Options:** file; negotiate a partial settlement; keep paying at real cost;
  keep paying and delay everything else.
- **What makes it hard:** the law offers the exit deliberately, and using a
  provision designed for you still means a promise you made is not being kept.

### 92. Money — Your child's deposit or your own old age

You can fund the house they cannot otherwise buy, and it comes out of the
years when you will have no way left to earn.

- **Options:** give it; lend it formally; guarantee the loan instead; keep it
  and say why.
- **What makes it hard:** helping now transfers a risk you can still absorb into
  years when you cannot — and the cost of that, if it lands, lands on them too,
  later and worse.

### 93. Money — Merging finances, or not

Joint account, separate accounts, or a proportional split. One of you earns
three times the other; one has debt from before.

- **Options:** pool everything; keep everything separate; pool proportionally to
  income; pool household costs only.
- **What makes it hard:** the arrangement that is fairest on paper is also the
  one that keeps a running account of who owes whom, in a relationship that is
  supposed not to have one.

## Health

*Source: `health-decisions.md`*

### 94. Health — The screening test that can only find something

The scan or blood test will detect abnormalities, many of which would never
have harmed you. Finding one starts a chain of biopsies, treatment, and years
of knowing.

- **Options:** screen on the recommended schedule; screen only with a specific
  reason; decline; screen and agree in advance not to act on ambiguous results.
- **What makes it hard:** you cannot tell in advance which finding is the one
  that would have killed you, so the same test that saves some people harms
  others — and you will never know which one you were.

### 95. Health — Whether to know

A genetic test would tell you your risk of a condition you can do little or
nothing to prevent. Your parent had it.

- **Options:** test and plan; test and tell no one; do not test; wait until
  there is something to be done about it.
- **What makes it hard:** the information changes nothing medical and everything
  else, and there is no way to sample it — you cannot find out a little and stop.

### 96. Health — Declining what the doctor recommends

The statin, the surgery, the referral. You have read enough to be uneasy and
not enough to be right, and they have seen a thousand of these.

- **Options:** follow the advice; decline and say why; ask for the evidence and
  decide; agree to a trial period with a defined review.
- **What makes it hard:** deferring to expertise is usually correct and is also
  how people end up on treatments nobody ever revisits — and your doctor is
  reasoning about a population, while you are the case.

### 97. Health — The second opinion

You could ask someone else. It costs time and money, may delay treatment, and
implies to the first doctor that you do not trust them.

- **Options:** ask openly; ask quietly elsewhere; accept the first opinion; ask
  only if the recommendation is irreversible.
- **What makes it hard:** seeking opinions until one agrees with you is a known
  failure mode, and so is accepting the first thing you were told by whoever
  happened to be on that day.

### 98. Health — Starting, or stopping, the psychiatric medication

It has helped. It also flattens something, or you no longer feel like the
person you were, or you simply want to know whether you still need it.

- **Options:** stay on it indefinitely; taper under supervision; stop and see;
  change to something else.
- **What makes it hard:** the faculty you would use to judge whether you still
  need it is the one the medication is acting on, and feeling well is both the
  evidence that it works and the reason people stop.

### 99. Health — The trial or the standard treatment

The experimental arm might be better, might be worse, and will produce
knowledge that helps people after you. Standard care is known and mediocre.

- **Options:** enroll; take standard care; take standard care and enroll if it
  fails; wait for the trial to report.
- **What makes it hard:** the trial only works because participants accept a
  worse expected outcome than a doctor choosing for them alone would allow —
  and someone has to, or nothing improves for anyone.

### 100. Health — Vaccination, when most of the benefit is other people's

Your own risk is low. The protection you would add is largely to people who
cannot be vaccinated or would not respond.

- **Options:** vaccinate; vaccinate only when locally advised; decline; decline
  and isolate when infectious.
- **What makes it hard:** your individual contribution to herd immunity is
  vanishingly small, and that is true of every single person in the population
  it depends on.

### 101. Health — Writing the advance directive

You are asked to specify now what should happen if you cannot speak later — how
much intervention, at what cost in suffering, at what threshold.

- **Options:** specify in detail; name a proxy and leave it to them; specify
  broad principles only; write nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** healthy people consistently say they would not want to
  live in conditions that people actually living in them consistently say they
  do — so you are binding a future self whose judgment may be better than yours.

### 102. Health — Who you tell

The diagnosis affects your work, your insurability, and how everyone treats
you. Disclosure buys accommodation and cannot be undone.

- **Options:** tell your employer; tell only HR or occupational health; tell
  family only; tell no one until you must.
- **What makes it hard:** protection generally requires disclosure, so the
  people best placed to accommodate you are the same people best placed to
  quietly stop relying on you.

### 103. Health — When someone you love refuses treatment

Your father will not see anyone. Your sister has stopped taking what keeps her
well. Both are, at least formally, competent to decide.

- **Options:** respect the refusal; keep pressing; involve their doctor;
  pursue a formal capacity assessment.
- **What makes it hard:** competence is the line the law draws and it is not
  where the difficulty is — the people hardest to watch refuse are usually
  competent, and being right about their interests gives you no standing at all.

## Friendship

*Source: `friendship-decisions.md`*

### 104. Friendship — Lending the money

They have asked for a real amount. You have it. They have been vague about
when it comes back and you both know the friendship is the collateral.

- **Options:** lend it; give it and say it is a gift; lend a smaller amount you
  are willing to lose; decline and offer another kind of help.
- **What makes it hard:** a loan makes them a debtor and a gift makes them a
  recipient, and neither is a thing a friendship easily contains — but refusing
  says something too.

### 105. Friendship — Telling them what you know about their partner

You saw something, or someone told you. They are happy. You might be wrong
about what it meant.

- **Options:** tell them plainly; tell them what you saw without interpreting;
  confront the partner first; say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** you would want to be told, which is the strongest
  argument and not evidence about them — and the messenger is frequently the
  only casualty.

### 106. Friendship — The friendship you are always the one to start

Every meeting for two years has begun with your message. They are warm when
you meet and never initiate.

- **Options:** keep initiating; stop and see what happens; name it directly;
  reduce it to the level they sustain.
- **What makes it hard:** effort is not evenly distributed in any friendship
  worth having, and there is no threshold anywhere that tells you when
  asymmetry has become an answer.

### 107. Friendship — The mistake you can see and they cannot

The marriage, the business, the move. You think it will go badly and they have
already decided and are telling you happily.

- **Options:** say it once, clearly; say it if asked; ask questions instead of
  objecting; support them and keep it to yourself.
- **What makes it hard:** they will remember what you said either way, and the
  friend who says nothing and the friend who says it once are indistinguishable
  right up until it goes wrong.

### 108. Friendship — Letting it drift, or naming it

You have not spoken properly in a year. Nothing happened. Getting back in touch
means acknowledging the gap; not doing it means the friendship quietly ends.

- **Options:** reach out as though nothing happened; reach out and name the
  gap; accept it has become something smaller; let it go.
- **What makes it hard:** friendships end almost entirely by attrition rather
  than decision, so the only way to lose one deliberately is to keep not acting.

### 109. Friendship — The friend who needs more than you have

The crisis is genuine and it has lasted eighteen months. The calls are long,
frequent, and one-directional, and you have started avoiding the phone.

- **Options:** keep going; say honestly what you can manage; help them find
  support that is not you; step back.
- **What makes it hard:** need does not distribute itself according to what
  anyone can supply, and the point at which you stop is the point at which they
  will remember you stopped.

### 110. Friendship — The secret you are keeping from your partner

Your friend told you something in confidence. You do not keep things from your
partner, and this is not yours.

- **Options:** keep it entirely; tell your partner and ask them to hold it; ask
  your friend for permission; tell your friend you cannot hold it.
- **What makes it hard:** both relationships make an implicit claim to complete
  candor, and you agreed to the second one without checking it against the
  first.

### 111. Friendship — Vouching for them

They have asked you to refer them for a job, or to introduce them to someone
who trusts you. You are fond of them and you have doubts about the work.

- **Options:** refer them warmly; refer them with a caveat; decline kindly;
  introduce without endorsing.
- **What makes it hard:** a recommendation spends credibility that is not
  really yours to spend, and declining tells your friend something about your
  opinion of them that you would never say aloud.

### 112. Friendship — The friend who has done something genuinely wrong

Not to you. To someone else, and it is serious, and they have not really
reckoned with it.

- **Options:** end the friendship; stay and say what you think; stay and say
  nothing; stay involved only on condition that they address it.
- **What makes it hard:** loyalty that survives nothing is not loyalty, and
  loyalty that survives everything is how people are enabled — and the person
  they wronged has no say in which one you are.

## Civic

*Source: `civic-decisions.md`*

### 113. Civic — The verdict the law requires

You are on the jury. The prosecution has proved its case and you think the law
itself, or the sentence that follows, is unjust.

- **Options:** convict as the law requires; acquit against the evidence; hold
  out and hang the jury; raise it with the judge.
- **What makes it hard:** you swore to apply the law, and the power to refuse is
  built into the jury on purpose — a power that is equally available to jurors
  whose convictions you would find monstrous.

### 114. Civic — Coming forward as a witness

You saw it. Giving a statement means being identified, being cross-examined
about your own recollection, and possibly being wrong in public.

- **Options:** give a full statement; give an anonymous tip; wait to be asked;
  say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** the whole system runs on ordinary people absorbing
  this cost, and the case where your account is decisive is also the case where
  being mistaken does the most damage.

### 115. Civic — Calling the police, when calling may make it worse

Something is happening — a fight, a person in crisis, a neighbor's child. The
response might help. It might also escalate into arrest, deportation, or
someone being hurt by the people arriving.

- **Options:** call; call a non-police crisis line; intervene yourself; wait and
  watch.
- **What makes it hard:** you are choosing on behalf of the person at risk, using
  a mechanism whose behavior you cannot predict and cannot recall once it is
  moving.

### 116. Civic — The gray area on the return

The home-office claim, the mileage, the classification that is defensible and
generous. It is not fabrication; it is where you resolve every ambiguity.

- **Options:** claim conservatively; claim what your accountant signs off;
  claim aggressively and expect to defend it; declare the doubt explicitly.
- **What makes it hard:** the rules are genuinely ambiguous and the person
  resolving every ambiguity in their own favor is not doing anything you could
  point to as the wrong step.

### 117. Civic — Paying cash and asking nothing

The builder wants cash. The price is meaningfully lower. What happens after
that is not, formally, your business.

- **Options:** insist on an invoice; pay cash and ask no questions; pay cash at
  the full price; use someone else.
- **What makes it hard:** you are not the one with the obligation to declare it,
  and you are the reason the arrangement is worth anything to either of you.

### 118. Civic — Someone you know has no legal status

Your colleague, your neighbor's husband, the person who cleans the office. You
know, and the law says something about what you know.

- **Options:** say nothing; help them get advice; employ or house them anyway;
  report it.
- **What makes it hard:** the duty to obey laws you disagree with is what makes
  law work at all, and this is a law whose enforcement falls entirely on a
  person you can see.

### 119. Civic — Reporting your father's driving

He is eighty-three, he has had two near misses, and driving is the last thing
keeping him independent. The licensing authority takes reports from family.

- **Options:** report him; ask his doctor to; persuade him to stop; do nothing
  yet.
- **What makes it hard:** the risk is to strangers who get no say, the cost is
  to someone you love and will have to keep facing, and nothing will happen for
  a long time — until it does.

### 120. Civic — Voting on what you know nothing about

The ballot has eleven positions. You have researched two. Judges, water boards,
coroners, a bond measure written by someone who did not want it understood.

- **Options:** vote the whole ballot on party or endorsement; leave the unknown
  ones blank; research everything; skip the election.
- **What makes it hard:** a vote cast on nearly no information is still a vote
  that counts the same, and abstaining hands those offices to the small number
  of people who have a specific interest in them.

### 121. Civic — The unpaid job nobody will take

The school board seat, the tenants' association, the committee. It will cost
you an evening a fortnight for three years and there is no one else.

- **Options:** take it; take it for one term only; help without holding the
  post; leave it vacant.
- **What makes it hard:** the argument that someone must do it is not an
  argument that it must be you, and it is the argument every single person
  declining is also using.

### 122. Civic — Filming it, and what to do next

You are recording something that should not be happening. The footage could be
evidence, or accountability, or a video of a bad moment in a stranger's life
that follows them forever.

- **Options:** film and give it only to those involved or an investigator; film
  and post it; stop filming and intervene; put the phone away.
- **What makes it hard:** the recording is often the only thing that makes
  anyone accountable, and publishing it takes the decision away from the person
  it was done to.

## Technology

*Source: `technology-privacy-decisions.md`*

### 123. Technology — The always-on microphone in a shared room

The speaker is genuinely useful. It sits in a kitchen where your family talks,
your friends visit, and your children's friends are looked after.

- **Options:** keep it and mute it selectively; keep it and tell visitors; move
  it somewhere private; do without.
- **What makes it hard:** you can consent for yourself in your own house, and
  everyone else in the room is subject to a decision they were never offered.

### 124. Technology — Handing over your address book

The app wants your contacts. Granting it improves the service immediately and
uploads the phone numbers of two hundred people who have not been asked.

- **Options:** grant it; grant it and delete afterward; decline and use the
  service without it; do not use the service.
- **What makes it hard:** the data is only valuable because it is about other
  people, and you are the only person in a position to give it away.

### 125. Technology — The genetic test that is also your relatives' data

Your sequence is largely your siblings', your parents', and your children's. A
match can identify a cousin who never took a test.

- **Options:** test with results kept private; test and opt out of matching;
  ask your immediate family first; do not test.
- **What makes it hard:** it is unambiguously your own body and unavoidably
  their information, and there is no version of consent that covers a family.

### 126. Technology — Reading your child's messages

You can see everything on their phone. They are thirteen. There is a real risk
you would catch, and a relationship you would be spending to catch it.

- **Options:** full visibility, disclosed; spot checks, disclosed; monitor
  covertly; do not monitor and talk instead.
- **What makes it hard:** the surveillance that would find the danger is the
  same surveillance that teaches them not to tell you things — and by the age
  where it matters most, they can route around it.

### 127. Technology — Location sharing that started as convenience

You and your partner share locations. It began as logistics. Now checking is
routine, and turning it off would itself be a statement.

- **Options:** keep it; turn it off mutually and say why; keep it and stop
  looking; make it temporary and opt-in.
- **What makes it hard:** it was agreed freely and is now a thing that cannot be
  withdrawn without meaning something, which is a strange property for consent
  to have.

### 128. Technology — Passing off what the machine wrote

The tool produced the memo, the code, the application essay. It is good. Nobody
asked how it was made and the answer would change how it is received.

- **Options:** disclose; use it for drafts and rewrite; use it where the output
  is what matters and not where the process is; do not use it.
- **What makes it hard:** the question of what "your own work" means is settled
  differently by different readers, and you are the only one who knows there is
  a question.

### 129. Technology — Building the thing that replaces your team

You have been asked to automate work currently done by eight people, some of
whom you like, all of whom will find out later.

- **Options:** build it; build it and argue for redeployment; tell your
  colleagues; decline.
- **What makes it hard:** the tool will exist whether or not you build it, and
  being the one who builds it is the only position from which the transition
  might be made survivable.
