# Ten Real-Life Decisions in Dating

Decisions made with incomplete information about another person, under time
pressure, where the honest option and the kind option frequently differ. Same
format as the companion files: the situation, the live options, and the tension
that keeps the choice from resolving.

---

## 1. When to disclose the significant thing

The bipolar diagnosis, the herpes, the debt, the criminal record, the fact that
you do not want children. Early is fair and invites a snap judgment; late feels
like something withheld.

- **Options:** put it in the profile; say it on the first date; wait until it is
  serious; wait until they ask.
- **What makes it hard:** disclosure exists to let someone consent to what they
  are getting into, and telling them before they know you at all guarantees the
  decision is made on the label rather than on you.

## 2. How much to look them up first

Ten minutes online would tell you their employer, their politics, their ex, and
possibly a court record. It is also a one-sided briefing they did not agree to.

- **Options:** a basic safety check only; a full search; nothing beyond the
  profile; search and say that you did.
- **What makes it hard:** meeting a stranger is a real risk and checking is
  prudent — and knowing things you have no ordinary way of knowing makes the
  first hour a performance of discovering them.

## 3. Seeing several people at once, and when to say so

Nobody has raised exclusivity. You are on second dates with two people and one
of them is clearly assuming otherwise.

- **Options:** raise it now; wait until someone asks; wait until it is serious
  with one; keep it to yourself as nobody's business yet.
- **What makes it hard:** the norm is genuinely unsettled, so acting on your own
  reading is fine right up until you discover theirs was different — and by then
  someone has already been hurt.

## 4. No spark yet, three dates in

They are kind, interesting, and available. You feel nothing much. People often
say attraction grew for them over months.

- **Options:** end it now; give it three more; end it and say why honestly;
  keep seeing them while looking around.
- **What makes it hard:** chemistry sometimes builds and sometimes never does,
  and staying to find out spends their time on a maybe you would not accept
  from someone else.

## 5. They do not want what you want

Six months in and going well. They are clear they do not want marriage, or
children, or to leave this city — and you do.

- **Options:** end it now; stay and hope it changes; stay knowing it will not;
  set a deadline and revisit.
- **What makes it hard:** people do change their minds, so hoping is not
  irrational — but organizing your life around someone changing is a way of not
  believing what they told you plainly.

## 6. Dating across a real values divide

They are good to you and they hold a political or religious position you find
seriously wrong, on something that is not abstract for you.

- **Options:** end it; stay and keep it out of bounds; stay and argue it out;
  stay and see whether either of you moves.
- **What makes it hard:** treating a conviction as a dealbreaker means treating
  people as their positions, and treating it as private means living with
  someone who would vote against something you need.

## 7. Your friend's ex, or your colleague

The connection is real. She dated your close friend for two years, or he sits
four desks away and you would both still be there afterward.

- **Options:** don't; ask your friend first; go ahead and tell them after; go
  ahead and say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** asking permission gives someone a veto over your life
  they should probably not have, and not asking treats their feelings as
  something to be managed after the fact.

## 8. Moving cities for someone

Eight months in. Their job cannot move and yours can. You would arrive with no
friends, no network, and a relationship that has never been tested by anything.

- **Options:** move; ask them to move; long distance with an end date; end it.
- **What makes it hard:** whoever moves carries the dependence, and the very
  thing that would tell you whether the relationship can bear that — time — is
  what the move is meant to shortcut.

## 9. An asymmetry you did not create

You earn four times what they do, or you are their manager's peer, or their
visa depends on staying in the country and you know it.

- **Options:** proceed as equals and ignore it; name it explicitly; change what
  you can (roles, money, housing); decline on principle.
- **What makes it hard:** consent given under dependence is still consent, and
  it is also the exact circumstance in which someone finds it hardest to say no
  — including to themselves.

## 10. Saying the true unkind thing, or fading out

You are not interested. The honest reason is something about them they cannot
change and would not enjoy hearing.

- **Options:** a brief honest message; a vague but clear ending; the full
  reason if asked; stop replying.
- **What makes it hard:** fading out spares them a bad five minutes and gives
  them nothing to close on, while the truth is useful only if they wanted it,
  and you cannot ask without delivering it.

---

## Why these ten

They separate the difficulties that get flattened into "just be honest":

- **Honesty against kindness** — 4 and 10 are the pure cases: the true thing is
  also the thing that helps nobody but your own conscience.
- **Information asymmetry** — 1, 2, and 3 all turn on who knows what, and when
  someone becomes entitled to know it.
- **Believing what you were told** — 5 and 6 both ask whether to plan around a
  person's stated position or around your hope about it.
- **Power that nobody chose** — 8 and 9 involve dependence that arises from
  circumstance rather than from anyone's conduct, which is what makes it hard
  to see and easy to exploit without meaning to.
