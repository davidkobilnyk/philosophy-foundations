# Ten Real-Life Decisions About Daily Life

Small, recurring decisions — the kind made weekly rather than once. Individually
each looks trivial; the difficulty is that the answer compounds over years.
Same format as the companion files: the situation, the live options, and the
tension that keeps the choice from resolving.

---

## 1. The phone in the bedroom

It is the alarm, the reading light, the thing you check at 6:40am before you
are properly awake, and the last hour of most evenings.

- **Options:** charge it in another room; keep it and use limits or greyscale;
  keep it and change nothing; a separate alarm clock and no phone after 10pm.
- **What makes it hard:** the cost is invisible per instance and only shows up
  in aggregate — and the device that would show you the aggregate is the one
  under examination.

## 2. Keeping a car

It costs a few hundred a month standing still. Transit would add forty minutes
a day but reclaim the parking, the insurance, and the maintenance.

- **Options:** keep it; sell it and use transit plus occasional rental; keep it
  and use it less; downsize to something cheaper.
- **What makes it hard:** the car buys time and buys freedom on the handful of
  days you really need it, and you cannot know in advance which days those are.

## 3. How much news to take in

Staying informed is a civic obligation of some kind. Six hours a week of it
leaves you anxious and no better able to act on any of it.

- **Options:** a daily briefing and nothing else; a weekly long-form roundup;
  follow only what you can act on; stay fully current.
- **What makes it hard:** the fraction of the news you can do anything about is
  tiny, but you cannot identify which fraction that is without reading it, and
  disengagement is exactly what makes people easy to govern badly.

## 4. Answering work messages after hours

Nobody has told you to. Replying at 9pm is noticed and rewarded. Not replying
is also noticed.

- **Options:** hard cutoff and say so; reply only to genuine emergencies; stay
  responsive; batch replies to one evening check.
- **What makes it hard:** a boundary only holds if you pay the cost of holding
  it, and the cost lands on you while the benefit of your availability is
  spread across people who will not remember it at review time.

## 5. Declining the invitation

You are tired. It is the third thing this week. You would enjoy it once you
were there, and you would also enjoy the evening at home.

- **Options:** go; decline honestly; decline with an excuse; go and leave early.
- **What makes it hard:** friendships are maintained by showing up more or less
  reliably, and the specific evening you skip is never the one that matters —
  until enough of them accumulate.

## 6. Whether to drink at all

Two or three drinks a week is socially frictionless and probably not costing
you much. Zero is cleaner, and makes every dinner a small conversation.

- **Options:** stop entirely; keep it to social occasions; set a weekly cap;
  change nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** the marginal harm at low volumes is genuinely
  uncertain, and abstaining has a social cost that falls entirely on you while
  the benefit is diffuse and statistical.

## 7. What to post about your family

The photos are good, the people who love your kids are far away, and your
children cannot consent to a permanent public record of their childhood.

- **Options:** post freely; a private list only; no faces or names; nothing at
  all, and send photos directly.
- **What makes it hard:** you are making a durable decision on behalf of someone
  who will have their own view about it in fifteen years, in exchange for
  something you enjoy now.

## 8. Cheap now or durable later

The £40 version will last two years. The £180 version will last fifteen, if you
still want it in fifteen years and if the claim is even true.

- **Options:** buy cheap and replace; buy the expensive one; buy secondhand;
  buy nothing and borrow.
- **What makes it hard:** buying well is cheaper over time only if you can
  afford the outlay and your tastes hold — and the pitch for durability is made
  by people selling durability.

## 9. Living alone or with other people

Alone is quiet, and yours, and expensive. Sharing halves the rent and fills the
kitchen with someone else's schedule.

- **Options:** live alone; share with friends; share with strangers; live with
  family.
- **What makes it hard:** solitude is restorative right up to the point where it
  becomes isolation, and the transition does not announce itself.

## 10. Where the exercise hour comes from

An hour of training has to displace an hour of something: sleep, work, the
family, the evening.

- **Options:** early morning at the cost of sleep; lunchtime at the cost of
  work; evening at the cost of family time; shorter sessions more often.
- **What makes it hard:** the return is real but slow and statistical, while
  everything it displaces makes its claim on you today, specifically, by name.

---

## Why these ten

They are chosen to show why small decisions resist being settled once:

- **The cost is invisible per instance** — 1, 5, and 10 only matter in
  aggregate, which is the hardest form of evidence to feel.
- **The benefit is diffuse and the cost is yours** — 4 and 6 both fail the way
  boundaries fail: alone.
- **Deciding for someone else** — 7 is the only one here where the person who
  bears the consequence is not in the room.
- **Uncertainty that will not resolve** — 2, 3, and 8 turn on predictions about
  your own future needs, and waiting for better information is itself a choice.
