# Nine Real-Life Decisions in Political Activism

Decisions that arise once you have decided the cause is right and the question
becomes what you are willing to do about it. Same format as the companion
files: the situation, the live options, and the tension that keeps the choice
from resolving.

---

## 1. Breaking a law you believe is unjust

The blockade, the occupation, the trespass. It would be effective, it is
illegal, and you have a job, a visa, or children who depend on you.

- **Options:** take the arrest openly; participate in ways that stay legal;
  support those who do it without doing it yourself; stay out.
- **What makes it hard:** civil disobedience draws its moral force from
  accepting the penalty publicly — which means the people best placed to be
  arrested are those with the least to lose, and their arrest carries the
  least weight.

## 2. Tactics that work by making people angry

Blocking a motorway, disrupting a performance, defacing a monument. Coverage is
enormous. Polling on your cause gets worse.

- **Options:** use disruptive tactics; use them only against the responsible
  institution; stick to persuasion and lobbying; do both through separate
  groups.
- **What makes it hard:** attention and sympathy are different currencies, and
  nobody can reliably tell you the exchange rate in advance — the historical
  cases are read both ways by serious people.

## 3. Voting for the candidate you can live with

Your preferred candidate cannot win. The one who can is materially better than
the alternative and has a record you find genuinely objectionable.

- **Options:** vote for the viable candidate; vote third party; abstain; vote
  viable but organize publicly against their record.
- **What makes it hard:** treating your vote as an instrument makes it almost
  worthless individually; treating it as a statement makes it worthless
  instrumentally. Both descriptions are true at once.

## 4. Money from a donor you would not defend

A large gift would fund three organizers for two years. The donor made the
money in a way your movement exists to oppose, or wants a say in strategy.

- **Options:** take it with conditions in writing; take it and disclose the
  source; refuse; take it and route it through an independent fund.
- **What makes it hard:** refusing keeps you clean and small. Accepting funds
  the work and gives your opponents an accurate story about who pays for you.

## 5. A coalition with people whose other positions you reject

To win this vote you need a bloc that agrees with you here and holds views you
find repugnant elsewhere. Without them the measure fails.

- **Options:** form the coalition openly; cooperate tacitly without joint
  appearances; refuse; work with them on this and campaign against them
  elsewhere.
- **What makes it hard:** coalitions launder reputations in both directions,
  and the people your alliance costs most are usually not the ones deciding
  whether to make it.

## 6. The compromise bill

The legislation delivers 40% of what you demanded, and its passage will drain
the urgency that made the other 60% possible. Opposing it means people go
without the 40% now.

- **Options:** back it and keep organizing; back it and say publicly it is
  inadequate; oppose it; stay neutral and let others decide.
- **What makes it hard:** you are weighing a certain, immediate, partial gain
  against a speculative larger one, and the people who need it now are not the
  same people who would benefit later.

## 7. Staying in an organization that has gone wrong

The leadership has become unaccountable, or has taken a position you think
betrays the point. You have ten years of relationships inside it.

- **Options:** organize internally for change; resign quietly; resign publicly
  with a statement; stay and dissent openly.
- **What makes it hard:** leaving publicly hands ammunition to opponents who
  want the whole thing discredited; staying quietly makes you a name on the
  masthead that lends it credibility.

## 8. Leaking what you agreed not to disclose

You have documents that would prove the thing your campaign has alleged for
years. You signed a confidentiality agreement, and the leak would be traced to
a small number of people.

- **Options:** leak anonymously; leak on the record; give it to a regulator;
  hold it and pursue lawful routes.
- **What makes it hard:** the promise was real and freely given, and keeping it
  protects a wrong that the promise was arguably designed to protect.

## 9. Organizing full time or earning and giving

You could organize for a modest salary, or take the high-paying job and fund
three organizers a year with the difference.

- **Options:** organize; earn and give; earn and give while volunteering;
  organize now and switch later.
- **What makes it hard:** money is fungible and your presence is not — but
  movements are made of people who stayed, and it is very easy to be persuaded
  that the comfortable option is the effective one.

---

## Why these nine

They separate the axes that arguments about activism usually run together:

- **Who pays for the tactic** — you (1), the public whose sympathy you need
  (2), or the people waiting on a benefit you might block (6).
- **Purity or effect** — 4, 5, and 9 are all versions of the same trade, and
  they come apart because the cost of staying clean falls on the cause rather
  than on you.
- **Promises against outcomes** — 8 pits an obligation you actually incurred
  against harm it would let continue.
- **Whether the effect is even knowable** — 2, 3, and 6 turn on causal claims
  about persuasion and momentum that nobody can settle in advance.
