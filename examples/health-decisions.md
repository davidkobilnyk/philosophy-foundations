# Ten Real-Life Decisions About Health

Decisions made on expert advice you cannot fully evaluate, on behalf of a
future self who will want different things than you want now. Same format as
the companion files: the situation, the live options, and the tension that
keeps the choice from resolving.

---

## 1. The screening test that can only find something

The scan or blood test will detect abnormalities, many of which would never
have harmed you. Finding one starts a chain of biopsies, treatment, and years
of knowing.

- **Options:** screen on the recommended schedule; screen only with a specific
  reason; decline; screen and agree in advance not to act on ambiguous results.
- **What makes it hard:** you cannot tell in advance which finding is the one
  that would have killed you, so the same test that saves some people harms
  others — and you will never know which one you were.

## 2. Whether to know

A genetic test would tell you your risk of a condition you can do little or
nothing to prevent. Your parent had it.

- **Options:** test and plan; test and tell no one; do not test; wait until
  there is something to be done about it.
- **What makes it hard:** the information changes nothing medical and everything
  else, and there is no way to sample it — you cannot find out a little and stop.

## 3. Declining what the doctor recommends

The statin, the surgery, the referral. You have read enough to be uneasy and
not enough to be right, and they have seen a thousand of these.

- **Options:** follow the advice; decline and say why; ask for the evidence and
  decide; agree to a trial period with a defined review.
- **What makes it hard:** deferring to expertise is usually correct and is also
  how people end up on treatments nobody ever revisits — and your doctor is
  reasoning about a population, while you are the case.

## 4. The second opinion

You could ask someone else. It costs time and money, may delay treatment, and
implies to the first doctor that you do not trust them.

- **Options:** ask openly; ask quietly elsewhere; accept the first opinion; ask
  only if the recommendation is irreversible.
- **What makes it hard:** seeking opinions until one agrees with you is a known
  failure mode, and so is accepting the first thing you were told by whoever
  happened to be on that day.

## 5. Starting, or stopping, the psychiatric medication

It has helped. It also flattens something, or you no longer feel like the
person you were, or you simply want to know whether you still need it.

- **Options:** stay on it indefinitely; taper under supervision; stop and see;
  change to something else.
- **What makes it hard:** the faculty you would use to judge whether you still
  need it is the one the medication is acting on, and feeling well is both the
  evidence that it works and the reason people stop.

## 6. The trial or the standard treatment

The experimental arm might be better, might be worse, and will produce
knowledge that helps people after you. Standard care is known and mediocre.

- **Options:** enroll; take standard care; take standard care and enroll if it
  fails; wait for the trial to report.
- **What makes it hard:** the trial only works because participants accept a
  worse expected outcome than a doctor choosing for them alone would allow —
  and someone has to, or nothing improves for anyone.

## 7. Vaccination, when most of the benefit is other people's

Your own risk is low. The protection you would add is largely to people who
cannot be vaccinated or would not respond.

- **Options:** vaccinate; vaccinate only when locally advised; decline; decline
  and isolate when infectious.
- **What makes it hard:** your individual contribution to herd immunity is
  vanishingly small, and that is true of every single person in the population
  it depends on.

## 8. Writing the advance directive

You are asked to specify now what should happen if you cannot speak later — how
much intervention, at what cost in suffering, at what threshold.

- **Options:** specify in detail; name a proxy and leave it to them; specify
  broad principles only; write nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** healthy people consistently say they would not want to
  live in conditions that people actually living in them consistently say they
  do — so you are binding a future self whose judgment may be better than yours.

## 9. Who you tell

The diagnosis affects your work, your insurability, and how everyone treats
you. Disclosure buys accommodation and cannot be undone.

- **Options:** tell your employer; tell only HR or occupational health; tell
  family only; tell no one until you must.
- **What makes it hard:** protection generally requires disclosure, so the
  people best placed to accommodate you are the same people best placed to
  quietly stop relying on you.

## 10. When someone you love refuses treatment

Your father will not see anyone. Your sister has stopped taking what keeps her
well. Both are, at least formally, competent to decide.

- **Options:** respect the refusal; keep pressing; involve their doctor;
  pursue a formal capacity assessment.
- **What makes it hard:** competence is the line the law draws and it is not
  where the difficulty is — the people hardest to watch refuse are usually
  competent, and being right about their interests gives you no standing at all.

---

## Why these ten

They separate the difficulties peculiar to health, as against the general
decisions elsewhere in the corpus:

- **Information you cannot hand back** — 1, 2, and 9 all turn on whether to
  learn or disclose something that cannot be unlearned or unsaid.
- **Deference and its limits** — 3, 4, and 6 each ask how much of your own
  judgment to hand to people who know more than you and are not you.
- **Your future self is not available for comment** — 5 and 8 both bind someone
  whose values you are guessing at, using faculties the decision itself affects.
- **Benefit that lands on other people** — 6, 7, and 10 place the gain
  somewhere other than with the person deciding.
