# Ten Real-Life Decisions About Hobbies

Decisions about the part of life that is chosen freely and justified by nothing
outside itself — which is what makes them surprisingly hard to reason about.
Same format as the companion files: the situation, the live options, and the
tension that keeps the choice from resolving.

---

## 1. Turning it into work

People are offering to pay. You could take commissions, sell the surplus, go
part-time at the job and make up the difference.

- **Options:** keep it unpaid; take occasional paid work; go semi-professional;
  make it the career.
- **What makes it hard:** the thing you love about it is that nobody is owed
  anything, and payment brings deadlines, customers, and the obligation to make
  the version that sells rather than the version you wanted.

## 2. The gear you might not use

The better instrument, the carbon frame, the proper kiln. It would remove a real
limitation, cost a month's savings, and might sit unused by March.

- **Options:** buy it now; buy secondhand; rent or borrow first; earn it by
  hitting a defined milestone.
- **What makes it hard:** equipment is the most reliable way to feel like you
  are progressing without progressing, and it is also sometimes exactly what is
  holding you back.

## 3. Competing or not

There are gradings, leagues, open calls, races. Entering would sharpen you fast
and turn the thing into a scoreboard.

- **Options:** compete seriously; enter occasionally for the deadline; set
  private goals instead; refuse the whole frame.
- **What makes it hard:** measurement is the fastest route to getting better and
  the most reliable route to only valuing what can be measured.

## 4. Quitting after twelve years

You no longer look forward to it. You are also good at it, known for it, and it
is part of how you and everyone else describe who you are.

- **Options:** stop; take an open-ended break; reduce it to something small;
  keep going and wait for it to come back.
- **What makes it hard:** identity outlives enthusiasm, so quitting costs
  something real that has nothing to do with whether you still enjoy it — and
  fallow periods are ordinary and do end.

## 5. The hours it takes from the people you live with

Six hours a week of training, or rehearsal, or the workshop. It is the thing
that makes you tolerable, and it is six hours nobody else in the house gets.

- **Options:** keep the time and defend it; halve it; move it to hours nobody
  wants anyway; involve them in it.
- **What makes it hard:** a person with nothing of their own is worse to live
  with, and that argument is also the most convenient thing you could possibly
  believe.

## 6. Shortcuts, and what counts as yours

The generated draft, the kit, the tracing, the pre-cut joinery, the assisted
edit. It gets you a better result faster and you did not do all of it.

- **Options:** avoid them entirely; use them and disclose; use them for the
  parts you do not care about; use whatever works and judge only the result.
- **What makes it hard:** if the point is the object, the shortcut is obviously
  fine; if the point is the doing, it is obviously not — and most people want
  both without having decided which is primary.

## 7. Posting it

You could put the work online. Feedback would improve it and an audience would
keep you going. It would also mean making things that do well online.

- **Options:** post everything; post selectively; share only with a small group;
  keep it private.
- **What makes it hard:** an audience is a genuine spur and a slow editor of
  what you attempt, and you will not notice it editing you.

## 8. The only club within thirty miles

It is the one place to do this locally. The culture is cliquey, or casually
unpleasant to newcomers, or run by someone nobody will confront.

- **Options:** join and keep your head down; join and try to change it; travel
  further; do it alone.
- **What makes it hard:** most hobbies need other people to be any good at all,
  and leaving costs you the activity while costing the culture nothing.

## 9. The risky one, now that people depend on you

Climbing, motorcycles, freediving, solo expeditions. The risk was always there
and now there is a child, or a partner who cannot sleep when you are out.

- **Options:** stop; reduce the risk with rules and equipment; keep going and
  insure heavily; keep going unchanged.
- **What makes it hard:** you are entitled to accept risk to your own life, and
  the consequences of the bad outcome fall almost entirely on people who did not
  choose it.

## 10. Teaching beginners or getting better yourself

The club needs someone to run the beginners' session. Those are the hours you
would otherwise spend improving, and you are at the point where improvement
still comes quickly.

- **Options:** teach; teach for a fixed term; decline and train; split the time.
- **What makes it hard:** the thing exists at all because someone did this for
  you, and there is no rule saying it has to be you, now, at the exact point it
  costs most.

---

## Why these ten

They show why decisions about optional things are not easy just because the
stakes are low:

- **Nothing external settles it** — 1, 3, and 6 all turn on what the activity is
  *for*, a question no result can answer from outside.
- **Motive is unreliable here** — 2, 5, and 7 are the cases where the
  self-serving answer and the correct answer are hardest to tell apart.
- **Who else absorbs it** — 5 and 9 spend other people's time, sleep, or safety
  on something only you chose.
- **Identity against enjoyment** — 4 and 8 both ask what you owe a version of
  yourself, or a community, that the present activity no longer repays.
