# Ten Real-Life Decisions About Civic and Legal Duty

Decisions where the obligation runs to strangers, routed through institutions
you did not design and cannot control once you have set them in motion. Same
format as the companion files: the situation, the live options, and the tension
that keeps the choice from resolving.

---

## 1. The verdict the law requires

You are on the jury. The prosecution has proved its case and you think the law
itself, or the sentence that follows, is unjust.

- **Options:** convict as the law requires; acquit against the evidence; hold
  out and hang the jury; raise it with the judge.
- **What makes it hard:** you swore to apply the law, and the power to refuse is
  built into the jury on purpose — a power that is equally available to jurors
  whose convictions you would find monstrous.

## 2. Coming forward as a witness

You saw it. Giving a statement means being identified, being cross-examined
about your own recollection, and possibly being wrong in public.

- **Options:** give a full statement; give an anonymous tip; wait to be asked;
  say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** the whole system runs on ordinary people absorbing
  this cost, and the case where your account is decisive is also the case where
  being mistaken does the most damage.

## 3. Calling the police, when calling may make it worse

Something is happening — a fight, a person in crisis, a neighbor's child. The
response might help. It might also escalate into arrest, deportation, or
someone being hurt by the people arriving.

- **Options:** call; call a non-police crisis line; intervene yourself; wait and
  watch.
- **What makes it hard:** you are choosing on behalf of the person at risk, using
  a mechanism whose behavior you cannot predict and cannot recall once it is
  moving.

## 4. The gray area on the return

The home-office claim, the mileage, the classification that is defensible and
generous. It is not fabrication; it is where you resolve every ambiguity.

- **Options:** claim conservatively; claim what your accountant signs off;
  claim aggressively and expect to defend it; declare the doubt explicitly.
- **What makes it hard:** the rules are genuinely ambiguous and the person
  resolving every ambiguity in their own favor is not doing anything you could
  point to as the wrong step.

## 5. Paying cash and asking nothing

The builder wants cash. The price is meaningfully lower. What happens after
that is not, formally, your business.

- **Options:** insist on an invoice; pay cash and ask no questions; pay cash at
  the full price; use someone else.
- **What makes it hard:** you are not the one with the obligation to declare it,
  and you are the reason the arrangement is worth anything to either of you.

## 6. Someone you know has no legal status

Your colleague, your neighbor's husband, the person who cleans the office. You
know, and the law says something about what you know.

- **Options:** say nothing; help them get advice; employ or house them anyway;
  report it.
- **What makes it hard:** the duty to obey laws you disagree with is what makes
  law work at all, and this is a law whose enforcement falls entirely on a
  person you can see.

## 7. Reporting your father's driving

He is eighty-three, he has had two near misses, and driving is the last thing
keeping him independent. The licensing authority takes reports from family.

- **Options:** report him; ask his doctor to; persuade him to stop; do nothing
  yet.
- **What makes it hard:** the risk is to strangers who get no say, the cost is
  to someone you love and will have to keep facing, and nothing will happen for
  a long time — until it does.

## 8. Voting on what you know nothing about

The ballot has eleven positions. You have researched two. Judges, water boards,
coroners, a bond measure written by someone who did not want it understood.

- **Options:** vote the whole ballot on party or endorsement; leave the unknown
  ones blank; research everything; skip the election.
- **What makes it hard:** a vote cast on nearly no information is still a vote
  that counts the same, and abstaining hands those offices to the small number
  of people who have a specific interest in them.

## 9. The unpaid job nobody will take

The school board seat, the tenants' association, the committee. It will cost
you an evening a fortnight for three years and there is no one else.

- **Options:** take it; take it for one term only; help without holding the
  post; leave it vacant.
- **What makes it hard:** the argument that someone must do it is not an
  argument that it must be you, and it is the argument every single person
  declining is also using.

## 10. Filming it, and what to do next

You are recording something that should not be happening. The footage could be
evidence, or accountability, or a video of a bad moment in a stranger's life
that follows them forever.

- **Options:** film and give it only to those involved or an investigator; film
  and post it; stop filming and intervene; put the phone away.
- **What makes it hard:** the recording is often the only thing that makes
  anyone accountable, and publishing it takes the decision away from the person
  it was done to.

---

## Why these ten

They separate the shapes of obligation that "civic duty" runs together:

- **The rule against the case in front of you** — 1, 4, and 6 all pit a rule
  that is right in general against a particular instance where it is not.
- **Setting something in motion you cannot control** — 3, 7, and 10 hand the
  outcome to an institution or an audience with its own momentum.
- **Costs to you, benefits to strangers** — 2 and 9 turn on whether an
  obligation that falls on everyone can fall on you specifically.
- **Defections that only matter in aggregate** — 4, 5, and 8 are individually
  negligible and collectively the whole problem.
