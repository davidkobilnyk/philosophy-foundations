# Seven Real-Life Decisions

A set of ordinary but consequential decisions people actually face. Each entry
states the situation, the live options, and what makes the choice hard — the
tension that keeps it from resolving into an obvious answer.

---

## 1. Taking the job that pays more but means moving away

You are offered a role in another city with a meaningful raise and better
long-term prospects. Your parents are aging nearby, and your closest friendships
are where you live now.

- **Options:** accept and move; decline and stay; negotiate remote or delayed start.
- **What makes it hard:** money and career growth are measurable; proximity to
  people you love is not. The costs of leaving arrive slowly and are hard to
  reverse.

## 2. Whether to have children, and when

You and your partner disagree about timing, or you are unsure whether you want
children at all. Fertility, finances, and career timelines all push in different
directions.

- **Options:** try now; wait a defined number of years; decide not to; freeze
  eggs or embryos to keep the option open.
- **What makes it hard:** you are choosing on behalf of a person who does not
  exist yet, and the preferences you will have afterward are not the ones you
  have now.

## 3. Moving a parent into assisted living

Your mother is becoming unsafe living alone, but insists she is fine and does
not want to leave her house.

- **Options:** respect her stated wish; move her against her objection; bring in
  in-home care; move her in with you.
- **What makes it hard:** her autonomy and her safety point opposite ways, and
  you are also weighing your own capacity without wanting to admit that you are.

## 4. Reporting a colleague's misconduct

You witness a coworker falsifying results, expensing personal costs, or
mistreating a junior employee. Reporting it will likely be traced back to you.

- **Options:** report through official channels; raise it with the person
  directly; tell a trusted manager informally; say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** the harm is real but diffuse, and the cost to you is
  concrete and personal. Loyalty to a person conflicts with loyalty to everyone
  the misconduct touches.

## 5. How aggressively to treat a serious illness

You or someone you are responsible for is diagnosed with a condition where
treatment offers a modest chance of more time at a significant cost in
suffering.

- **Options:** pursue aggressive treatment; choose palliative care; try one
  round and reassess.
- **What makes it hard:** more time and better time are different goods, and
  the person best placed to weigh them may be too sick or too frightened to
  decide.

## 6. Cutting off a family member

A sibling or parent has repeatedly caused you harm and shows no sign of
changing. Contact costs you something real each time.

- **Options:** cut contact entirely; set strict limited contact; keep trying;
  attempt a mediated conversation.
- **What makes it hard:** obligations to family are not chosen, and the door you
  close may not reopen. You are also deciding for others in the family who will
  have to take sides.

## 7. Leaving a stable career to do the thing you actually want

You have savings for two years and a skill you have never tested commercially.
Staying is safe and quietly deadening.

- **Options:** quit and commit; go part-time and build on the side; set a
  savings threshold and a deadline; stay.
- **What makes it hard:** you cannot learn whether it would work without giving
  up the thing that makes failure survivable.

---

## Why these seven

They vary along several dimensions on purpose:

- **Reversibility** — a job move can be undone; declining a life-extending
  treatment cannot.
- **Who bears the cost** — some fall mainly on you (7), others mainly on
  someone else (3, 5).
- **Whether the stakes are knowable** — a salary difference is arithmetic under
  uncertainty; whether to have a child is not arithmetic at all.
- **Whose values govern** — several involve deciding for a person whose own
  wishes conflict with their interests (3, 5).
