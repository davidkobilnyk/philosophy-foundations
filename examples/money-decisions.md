# Ten Real-Life Decisions About Money

Decisions where, unusually, the odds can be roughly estimated — and knowing
them still does not settle it. Same format as the companion files: the
situation, the live options, and the tension that keeps the choice from
resolving.

---

## 1. How much insurance to carry

Every policy is, on average, a losing bet: the premium exceeds the expected
payout, or the insurer would not sell it. You are choosing how much to lose on
purpose.

- **Options:** insure heavily; insure only against ruin; take the high
  deductible and self-insure the rest; skip the optional cover entirely.
- **What makes it hard:** the correct amount depends on which losses would end
  you rather than merely hurt, and people are systematically bad at telling
  those apart until they are in one.

## 2. Paying down the debt or investing the difference

The loan is at 4%. The market has historically returned more. Clearing it is
guaranteed; the spread is not.

- **Options:** overpay the debt; invest and keep the debt; split; clear the
  high-interest debt only and invest the rest.
- **What makes it hard:** the arithmetic favors investing and the arithmetic
  assumes you will hold your nerve through the years where it does not work —
  which is a claim about you, not about markets.

## 3. How much risk, in a life you only run once

The aggressive allocation has the better expected outcome across many
lifetimes. You get one, and it has to cover a specific set of years.

- **Options:** maximize expected return; hold a large safe allocation; glide
  from one to the other with age; decide by what you could stand to lose.
- **What makes it hard:** expected value is the right guide for a bet you can
  repeat, and nothing about a retirement is repeatable.

## 4. The emergency fund that is quietly shrinking

Six months of expenses in cash, losing to inflation every year, doing nothing —
except the one thing it is for.

- **Options:** hold six months; hold three and a credit line; invest it and
  accept the sequence risk; size it to your actual job security.
- **What makes it hard:** the cost is continuous and visible while the benefit
  is a single event that may never come, which is exactly the shape of decision
  people abandon just before they need it.

## 5. Cash or equity

The startup offers a lower salary and a slice that is probably worth nothing
and might be worth a house.

- **Options:** take the cash; take the equity; take a mix; take the cash and
  invest the difference yourself.
- **What makes it hard:** the equity's expected value can be genuinely high
  while its median outcome is zero, and you cannot diversify a bet that is also
  your employer, your salary, and your next reference.

## 6. The lump sum or the income for life

Take the pension as a single payment you control, or as a payment that arrives
every month until you die.

- **Options:** take the lump sum; take the annuity; take part of each; delay
  and decide later.
- **What makes it hard:** the annuity is insurance against living a long time,
  which is not a risk anyone feels — and the lump sum is worth more on a
  spreadsheet than in the hands of a person who will be eighty-five.

## 7. Money you can afford to lose

The single stock, the crypto, the friend's venture. You have set aside an
amount whose loss would not hurt you.

- **Options:** don't; a capped percentage; put it in and forget it; keep adding
  while it works.
- **What makes it hard:** "money I can afford to lose" is defined before the
  loss and revised after it, and the position that stays small is not the one
  that has been going up.

## 8. Walking away from a debt you legally owe

Bankruptcy, or a strategic default, or simply stopping. It is lawful, it is
what the system provides for, and someone will not be paid.

- **Options:** file; negotiate a partial settlement; keep paying at real cost;
  keep paying and delay everything else.
- **What makes it hard:** the law offers the exit deliberately, and using a
  provision designed for you still means a promise you made is not being kept.

## 9. Your child's deposit or your own old age

You can fund the house they cannot otherwise buy, and it comes out of the
years when you will have no way left to earn.

- **Options:** give it; lend it formally; guarantee the loan instead; keep it
  and say why.
- **What makes it hard:** helping now transfers a risk you can still absorb into
  years when you cannot — and the cost of that, if it lands, lands on them too,
  later and worse.

## 10. Merging finances, or not

Joint account, separate accounts, or a proportional split. One of you earns
three times the other; one has debt from before.

- **Options:** pool everything; keep everything separate; pool proportionally to
  income; pool household costs only.
- **What makes it hard:** the arrangement that is fairest on paper is also the
  one that keeps a running account of who owes whom, in a relationship that is
  supposed not to have one.

---

## Why these ten

They isolate what makes money decisions different from the rest of the corpus:

- **Odds you can actually estimate** — 1, 3, and 7 are the rare cases where the
  probabilities are roughly knowable and the answer still is not obvious.
- **You only get one run** — 3, 4, and 6 all fail if you reason as though the
  bet were repeatable, which is exactly how the arithmetic is usually presented.
- **Where the risk goes when you shed it** — 1 and 8 both move risk onto someone
  else, once by contract and once by default; only one of them is priced.
- **Money standing in for something else** — 9 and 10 are settled arithmetic and
  unsettled anyway, because the number is not what is being argued about.
