# Ten Real-Life Decisions at Work

Decisions inside an institution that pays you, ranks you, and can end your
income — where your interests, your colleagues', and the organization's come
apart in ways nobody says out loud. Same format as the companion files: the
situation, the live options, and the tension that keeps the choice from
resolving.

---

## 1. Telling colleagues what you earn

You have discovered you are paid considerably more than someone doing the same
work, or considerably less. Saying so would change what everyone can negotiate.

- **Options:** tell the person affected; tell the team; raise it with your
  manager instead; say nothing.
- **What makes it hard:** secrecy about pay serves the employer almost entirely,
  and breaking it hands someone a grievance they cannot unlearn and may not be
  able to act on.

## 2. Negotiating hard from a fixed pool

You have an outside offer and real leverage. The raise budget for your team is
capped, and what you win comes out of what is left for four other people.

- **Options:** negotiate to the maximum; take a fair number and leave room;
  negotiate hard and push for the pool to be raised; accept what is offered.
- **What makes it hard:** you are the only person in the room with an interest
  in your salary, and the zero-sum framing is one your employer chose and
  benefits from.

## 3. The honest reference

A former report you had to manage out asks you for a reference. They tried
hard, they were not good at the job, and they need work.

- **Options:** decline to give one; give a narrow factual confirmation; give a
  warm reference; be candid about the fit.
- **What makes it hard:** a reference is only worth anything if it is honest,
  and the cost of your honesty falls on one identifiable person while the
  benefit is spread across a hiring process that will never know it.

## 4. Firing someone who is trying

Six months of support, a clear plan, visible effort, and the work is still not
good enough. They have a mortgage and they know it is going badly.

- **Options:** terminate now; extend the plan; move them to a role that fits;
  keep them and absorb the gap.
- **What makes it hard:** effort is the thing you spend a career telling people
  to give, and it is not what the job is actually paid for — and every month you
  delay is a month they are not looking elsewhere.

## 5. Credit for shared work

The idea was yours; the execution was collective; the write-up went out under
someone else's name. Pushing for attribution makes you the person who pushes
for attribution.

- **Options:** raise it privately with them; raise it with your manager; correct
  the record publicly; let it go.
- **What makes it hard:** credit compounds into promotions and pay, so letting
  it go is not costless — and the people who most need the credit are usually
  the ones who look worst asking for it.

## 6. Telling your manager you are interviewing

You are two rounds into a process elsewhere. Your manager is about to staff you
onto a year-long project, and has been decent to you.

- **Options:** tell them now; tell them when you have an offer; say nothing and
  decline the project vaguely; take the project and leave if it comes through.
- **What makes it hard:** honesty here is unilateral — they would not tell you
  in advance that your role was being cut, and disclosing early can make the
  decision for you.

## 7. Covering for a teammate in crisis

Their work has slipped badly for two months. There is a divorce, or an illness.
You have been quietly redoing parts of it.

- **Options:** keep covering; tell them you cannot continue; tell your manager;
  ask them what they want you to do.
- **What makes it hard:** covering protects them now and delays the help that
  actually fits — and the person paying for it, invisibly, is you.

## 8. Building the thing that is legal and bad

The cancellation flow with the buried button, the metric everyone knows
overstates the result, the fee disclosed on page eleven. Nothing here is
unlawful.

- **Options:** build it; build it and record your objection; propose a version
  you can defend; refuse.
- **What makes it hard:** refusing on your own achieves nothing but replacement
  by someone quieter, and "it is legal" is doing an enormous amount of work in
  a sentence nobody wants to finish.

## 9. The promotion into management

More money, more security, and an end to the work you are actually good at and
enjoy. Declining it marks you as someone with no ambition.

- **Options:** take it; decline and stay technical; take it for a fixed trial;
  ask for a senior individual track instead.
- **What makes it hard:** organizations mostly offer one ladder, so refusing the
  role often means refusing the pay — and you cannot find out whether you would
  be good at it without giving up the thing you would return to.

## 10. Owning the mistake nobody noticed

Your error cost real money, or shipped a wrong number to a client. It has not
been traced to you and probably never will be.

- **Options:** report it immediately; fix it quietly and say nothing; tell your
  manager privately; disclose it to whoever was affected.
- **What makes it hard:** disclosure is straightforwardly right and lands
  entirely on you, and the strongest argument for silence — that the harm is
  already done and confessing helps nobody — is the same argument available to
  everyone who has ever concealed anything.

---

## Why these ten

They separate the pressures that get flattened into "office politics":

- **The rules are set by one party** — 1, 2, and 6 all involve norms of secrecy
  or loyalty that hold in one direction only.
- **Kindness now against help later** — 3, 4, and 7 each buy someone comfort in
  the short term at a cost they will pay without seeing it.
- **What individual refusal actually achieves** — 8 is the clearest case: the
  thing gets built either way, so the question is what your participation
  itself amounts to.
- **Costs that land wholly on you** — 5, 9, and 10 all pit an identifiable
  personal cost against a diffuse or purely internal benefit.
